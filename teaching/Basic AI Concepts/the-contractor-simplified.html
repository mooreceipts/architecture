A beginner's guide to working with AI

The Contractor

A practical model for understanding prompts, context, tools, agents, and review—without requiring a technical background.

Think of an AI model as a capable new contractor.

It can help immediately, but it does not automatically know your goal, private information, standards, or what a correct result looks like. Better work comes from clear instructions, relevant context, approved tools, appropriate autonomy, and review.

Start here

Three habits that improve your next conversation

These matter whether you use a phone app, website, or advanced workspace.

01 · The task brief and runbook

Give clear instructions

Prompt + skill

What it means

A prompt is the brief for one interaction. A skill or runbook is a reusable set of instructions, examples, and checks for work you perform repeatedly.

Why it matters

When a request is ambiguous, the model must guess. State the outcome, relevant background, audience, constraints, and what success looks like. A short prompt can work when the task is already clear; detail is useful when it removes uncertainty.

Try this

VagueWrite an email about the launch delay.

ClearerDraft a 120-word email to beta customers explaining a two-week launch delay. Use a calm, direct tone. Avoid assigning blame. End with the revised date. Before drafting, ask up to three questions about missing facts.

02 · The current project packet

Provide relevant context

Context window

What it means

The context window is the information a model can consider during the current interaction: your messages, its replies, system instructions, and any supplied files or tool results.

Why it matters

Context is working material, not guaranteed permanent memory. Some apps save chat history or offer memory features, but behavior varies. Never assume the model remembers information from another session.

More material is not always better. Supply the few sources that matter, identify why each matters, and remove outdated or conflicting material.

Try this

Say: “Use only the attached policy and price sheet. The policy controls eligibility; the price sheet controls amounts. If either source lacks an answer, identify the missing information instead of guessing.”

03 · Review before release

Check important work

Verification

What it means

Models generate plausible outputs and can include unsupported details, bad calculations, broken citations, or misunderstood requirements. Clear instructions reduce errors; they do not guarantee correctness.

Why it matters

Match review effort to consequence. A brainstorming list needs little review. Medical, legal, financial, security, publication, or system-changing work needs authoritative sources and qualified human judgment.

Check four things
  • Facts and citations match original sources.
  • Calculations can be reproduced.
  • Output follows every stated constraint.
  • Any action affects only intended people, files, or systems.

Next level

Capability, access, and independence

These concepts explain why the same request behaves differently across AI products and workflows.

04 · The underlying capability

Choose the model after defining the job

Model

What it means

The model is the trained system underneath the app. Different models vary in speed, cost, input types, reasoning performance, writing style, and how much context they can handle.

Why it matters

A stronger model may help with difficult work, but missing instructions, missing evidence, or missing access can still limit it. Define the task and evaluation method before comparing models.

Try this

Test candidates with the same real task and the same scoring checklist. Compare correctness, usefulness, time, and cost—not only how confident the answer sounds.

05 · The workbench and access badge

Give only the tools and access required

Platform + connectors

What it means

The platform is the environment around the model: chat interface, file workspace, code runner, browser, or other tools. Connectors link that environment to approved applications and data. MCP is one standard used to build such connections.

Why it matters

Tools can let AI inspect real documents, search approved sources, calculate, create files, or take actions. A connection is not universal access; permissions still control what it can see and do.

Try this

Grant the smallest useful scope. Prefer read-only access first. Name approved sources, forbidden actions, confirmation points, and the condition that means “stop and ask.”

06 · A longer work loop

Choose an appropriate level of autonomy

Agent

What it means

Chat usually handles one exchange at a time. An agent can pursue an objective through several steps: make a plan, use tools, observe results, adjust, and continue until it finishes or becomes blocked.

Why it matters

A longer leash saves effort on bounded, testable work. It also increases the effect of vague goals, excessive permissions, and weak stop conditions. Multiple agents add coordination costs as well as capacity.

Try this

Define the goal, allowed tools, limits, success test, and escalation rule. Start with a reversible task and require approval before publishing, purchasing, deleting, messaging, or changing important systems.

A reliable six-step workflow

Do not search for one perfect prompt. Treat useful AI work as a short collaboration and review loop.

  1. Define the outcomeState who needs what, why, and what success looks like.
  2. Invite questionsLet the model identify missing facts before producing work.
  3. Supply contextProvide relevant sources, constraints, and examples.
  4. Plan, then draftFor complex work, inspect the approach before the full output.
  5. Critique and reviseAsk what may be wrong, weak, missing, or unsupported.
  6. Verify and saveCheck the result. Turn successful repeated steps into a runbook.

Troubleshooting

When results disappoint

Diagnose the missing ingredient before adding more words or switching products.

“The answer is generic.”

Likely gap: unclear audience, goal, or source material. Add relevant context and a concrete success test.

“It invented a detail.”

Likely gap: missing evidence or weak verification. Require source-backed claims, mark uncertainty, then check originals.

“It forgot earlier information.”

Likely gap: information fell outside available context or was never saved. Restate the critical facts or attach a concise project brief.

“It cannot complete the task.”

Likely gap: missing tool, permission, or supported action. Check the platform before rewriting the prompt.

“The agent wandered.”

Likely gap: vague goal, no checkpoints, or weak stop conditions. Narrow the task and require staged approval.

“A stronger model did not help.”

Likely gap: model capability was not the bottleneck. Recheck instructions, context, access, and evaluation.

Before connecting anything

Use access deliberately

Convenience does not remove responsibility. Match permissions and oversight to possible harm.

Protect information

Do not provide confidential, personal, regulated, or credential data unless policy and product controls allow it.

Limit permissions

Connect only required systems and data. Prefer narrow, temporary, and read-only access where possible.

Confirm consequences

Require human approval before external messages, purchases, publication, deletion, or important system changes.

Keep evidence

Retain source links, test results, change previews, and other records needed to review important work.

Where the comparison stops

The contractor is a teaching metaphor, not a description of consciousness or human behavior. AI does not become loyal, nervous, offended, or personally invested in the work.

Models learn statistical patterns during training; they do not automatically learn your organization while chatting. Product features may save history or memory, so check the specific tool instead of assuming information will persist.
