A beginner's guide to what's happening inside AI

LLMs, Demystified

A plain-language look at how large language models turn text into tokens, learn patterns, generate replies, and sometimes get things wrong — no computer science degree required.

An LLM is a giant next-piece-of-text prediction system.

Given the text so far, it calculates probabilities for possible next pieces, selects one, adds it to the sequence, and repeats. The model itself does not search a database, but the AI product around it may provide documents, memory, web search, calculators, or other tools.

Inside a reply

From an AI product to one predicted token

Four ideas explain what receives your prompt, how text becomes numbers, and how those numbers become a reply.

01 · The model is only one part

Separate the model from the product

LLM + surrounding system

What it means

The LLM is the trained prediction system. The chat app or AI product around it may add system instructions, saved memory, uploaded files, web search, calculators, safety rules, and other tools. Those additions can make the same underlying model behave very differently.

What you see: chat or AI app
Optional tools: search, files, memory, calculators
Current instructions and context
Underlying LLM: predicts tokens

Why it matters

“The AI searched the web,” “the app remembered me,” and “the model knew it” describe different mechanisms. A model alone generates from its training and current context. A product can fetch fresh information and place it into that context before asking the model to answer.

02 · Chopping language into reusable pieces

Reading in tokens, not words

Tokenization

What a token is

Before text reaches the model, a tokenizer breaks it into tokens and assigns each token a number. A token may be a whole word, part of a word, punctuation, whitespace, a byte sequence, or a special control marker. The model processes those numbered pieces, not the page exactly as you see it.

Why tokenizers differ

A tokenizer has a fixed vocabulary built for a particular model family and training mixture. Frequently occurring character sequences may earn one compact token; rarer sequences must be assembled from smaller pieces. Different designers choose different algorithms, vocabulary sizes, training data, language coverage, code coverage, and special markers. Even one provider may use different tokenizers for different model families or generations.

The tokenizer and model are trained to work together. Token number 12,345 might mean one text fragment to Model A and something unrelated to Model B. Swapping tokenizers would scramble the model's learned input meanings.

Visible text unbelievable results!
Tokenizer A: 3 unbelievable␠results!
Tokenizer B: 5 unbelievable␠results!

Illustrative splits only. Exact pieces depend on the selected model's tokenizer. The ␠ symbol makes a leading space visible; real tokenizer displays use different conventions.

Why token counts change

The same sentence can use different token counts across models. A tokenizer trained with more examples from a language or programming syntax may represent its common patterns more compactly. Names, uncommon words, emoji, formatting, and mixed-language text may split differently as well. Chat products can also add system instructions, tool descriptions, and special formatting tokens that are not visible in your typed prompt.

Token counts are not directly comparable

A token is not a standard-sized unit. Think of grocery bags: one store might pack the same groceries into three large bags while another uses five smaller ones. Likewise, different models can divide identical text into different numbers of tokens. “One million tokens” does not guarantee the same amount of text, information, computation, quality, or price across models — even models from the same provider.

  • Text amount differs: each tokenizer may split identical text into a different number of pieces.
  • Meaning differs: token IDs and vocabulary entries are model-specific.
  • Work differs: architectures and reasoning methods may spend different computation on each visible input or output token.
  • Billing differs: providers set model-specific rates and may price input, cached input, output, or internal reasoning differently.

Use tokens to measure limits and cost within the named model and pricing rules. Use words, characters, documents, task results, and total price when comparing across models.

03 · Deciding what in the prompt matters now

Connecting tokens through attention

Transformer + attention

What it means

Tokens are converted into lists of numbers that represent learned features. These numbers pass through many transformer layers. Within those layers, attention lets each token weigh which earlier tokens are most relevant to its current job.

Why it matters

Attention is why an LLM can do much more than a phone's next-word suggestions. When resolving “it” in a paragraph, matching an answer to a question, or keeping a style consistent, the model can draw on relationships across much of the available context. Think of adjustable highlighting, not conscious focus: many patterns are calculated at once.

04 · One probability choice, repeated

Predicting the next token

Next-token prediction

What it means

After processing the context, the model assigns probabilities to many possible next tokens. A decoding method selects or samples one candidate, appends it to the context, then runs the process again. Thousands of small choices can become one long answer.

Illustrative next-token probabilities

After “The cat sat on the …” a model might assign:

mat48%
floor22%
chair11%
moon<1%

Made-up percentages for teaching. Real candidate lists are far larger.

Why it matters

The model does not always choose the single highest-probability token. Sampling settings can allow less likely choices, producing variety between runs. This probability process can produce useful planning and reasoning behavior, but polished language alone does not prove that a claim is true.

How it got that way

Training, scale, context, and reliability

These concepts explain how a prediction system becomes an assistant, what model size means, and where fluent output can mislead.

05 · Learning language, then learning assistant behavior

Training happens in stages

Pretraining + post-training

Pretraining: learn broad patterns

Developers collect, filter, and prepare a very large mixture of text and other data. During pretraining, the model repeatedly predicts a hidden next token. Its prediction is compared with the real token, and its parameters are adjusted slightly to reduce future error. Repeating this across enormous datasets builds patterns for language, facts, style, code, and problem-solving.

Post-training: shape an assistant

A raw pretrained model is good at continuing text but may not be helpful in conversation. Post-training uses examples, human or automated preferences, safety training, and tests to improve instruction-following and response behavior. This stage helps explain why two models with similar size can feel very different.

Why it matters

Training data leaves fingerprints: strengths, gaps, cultural assumptions, outdated information, and bias can all carry into outputs. The model does not contain a clean, searchable copy of its training set, but it can sometimes reproduce memorized fragments. Most knowledge is spread across many learned parameter patterns rather than stored as readable documents.

Think of it like

Think of learning a language in two stages: first seeing many examples, then practicing conversations with feedback. Pretraining builds broad patterns; post-training practices how to respond to requests. Neither stage guarantees that every remembered detail is correct.

06 · The dials tuned during training

What “7B” or “70B” actually counts

Parameters

What it means

Parameters are internal settings adjusted during training. Together, they shape how inputs become predictions. When a published model name includes 7B or 70B, it usually means roughly 7 billion or 70 billion parameters. No single parameter is normally “the Paris fact” or “the grammar setting”; useful behavior emerges from many numbers working together.

Why it matters

Within a comparable model family, more parameters often provide more capacity for subtle patterns. Across different families, parameter count is not an intelligence score. Architecture, training data, training compute, post-training, and task fit can let a smaller model outperform a larger one.

More parameters usually require more memory and computation, but parameter count is not the same as download size. Numerical precision and compression change how many bytes each parameter occupies. Some mixture-of-experts models also report both total parameters and the smaller number active for each token, so compare like with like.

Think of it like

A recipe with billions of tiny adjustable amounts. More adjustments can allow greater nuance, but ingredients and preparation still matter. A longer recipe does not automatically make a better meal.

07 · The workspace used for this reply

Working with what fits in context

Context window

What it means

The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can consider during one generation. System instructions, conversation messages, attached or retrieved material, tool results, and the answer as it grows may all consume space in that budget.

Why it matters

A bigger context window provides more workspace, not guaranteed memory or attention. Information can fit inside the window and still be overlooked, especially inside a long, cluttered prompt. When the space fills, an app may reject more input, remove older material, summarize it, or start a fresh context. Product memory features work by saving selected information and supplying it again later.

Think of it like

A table with limited space. Material placed on the table is available to use; material stored elsewhere is not. A crowded table also makes important items easier to overlook.

08 · Fluency is not proof

Why plausible answers can be wrong

Hallucination + sampling

What it means

A model generates a likely continuation; it does not automatically check each sentence against reality. When learned patterns are incomplete, conflicting, outdated, or unsupported by current evidence, it can create a fluent but false detail — commonly called a hallucination. Search, retrieval, calculators, and citations can ground an answer, but their results still need correct interpretation.

Why answers can vary

Decoding settings control how the system selects among probable next tokens. Temperature is one setting that usually increases variety as it rises. Lower temperature can make output more consistent, but it does not turn prediction into fact-checking and does not guarantee truth. Many consumer apps choose these settings for you.

Why it matters

Fluency reflects skill at producing coherent language, not a calibrated certainty meter. Verify unfamiliar or consequential claims against reliable sources, especially quotations, citations, calculations, dates, and current events.

Think of it like

Someone giving directions from memory without checking a map. The explanation may sound clear and confident while still including a wrong turn. Supplying a reliable map helps, but the route should still be checked.

Two timelines, not one

Training creates and shapes a model. Inference is what happens later when that trained model generates your reply.

How a model is made

Usually performed by the model developer before you use it.

  1. Collect and prepareData is gathered, filtered, cleaned, mixed, and often deduplicated.
  2. Build a tokenizerA vocabulary maps recurring text pieces to numerical IDs.
  3. PretrainNext-token errors repeatedly adjust parameters across enormous datasets.
  4. Post-trainExamples, preferences, and safety methods shape assistant behavior.
  5. EvaluateDevelopers test capability, reliability, safety, speed, and cost.

How your reply is generated

Called inference: using the trained model without retraining it.

  1. Assemble contextThe product combines instructions, messages, files, memory, and tool results.
  2. TokenizeThe model's tokenizer converts that context into token IDs.
  3. Process relationshipsTransformer layers and attention calculate contextual representations.
  4. Score candidatesThe model assigns probabilities to possible next tokens.
  5. Select and repeatOne token is chosen, appended, then used to predict the next.
  6. Stop and decodeA stop signal or limit ends generation; token IDs become readable output.

Common misconceptions

Myth vs. reality

Where everyday intuition about AI tends to go wrong.

“It is a person thinking in sentences.”

Reality: underneath the conversational surface are numerical representations, transformer layers, and repeated token predictions. Human-sounding output is not evidence of human experience or human-style understanding.

“It has a photographic memory of the internet.”

Reality: training spreads patterns across parameters rather than creating a searchable archive. Models can misquote, blend sources, and occasionally reproduce memorized fragments.

“Bigger means smarter at every task.”

Reality: parameter count is one ingredient. Training, architecture, tools, speed, cost, and task fit can matter more.

“A large context window means perfect memory.”

Reality: it measures what can fit, not what will be used perfectly. Saved memory is a separate product feature that supplies selected information again.

“A token is the same unit in every model.”

Reality: tokenizer vocabularies differ. Identical text may use different pieces and counts, so tokens are not a universal measure of text, work, quality, or price.

“If it sounds confident, it is probably right.”

Reality: fluent wording is what the generation process produces whether a claim is supported or invented. Verify anything consequential.

Try three quick experiments

No technical tools needed. Each activity takes about a minute and shows one idea from this guide.

1. Notice how earlier words change the next guess

  1. Finish this sentence three ways: “On the kitchen floor, the mouse moved toward the ___.”
  2. Now finish this one: “On the computer screen, the mouse moved toward the ___.”
  3. Compare your answers. The word “mouse” stayed the same, but the surrounding words changed what seemed likely.

What this shows: an LLM uses the surrounding text when predicting what comes next. It is not choosing from one fixed answer.

2. Watch the same text split differently

  1. Open the OpenAI Tokenizer in a new tab.
  2. Select Show example. You can also replace the example with these four sentences:

The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.
Unbelievable results require careful checking.
¿Dónde está la biblioteca más cercana?
Please rename customer_id to customerAccountId 🚀.

  1. Select each model group in turn: GPT-5.x & O1/3, GPT-4 & GPT-3.5 (legacy), then GPT-3 (legacy).
  2. Watch the colored text pieces and the token total as you switch between the three choices.
  3. Look for words that stay whole in one choice but break into smaller pieces in another. Notice what happens to punctuation, Spanish text, the underscore, and the emoji.

What this shows: the text stays the same, but the pieces and total can change because model generations use different tokenization rules. A count such as “100 tokens” only has meaning when you also know which model or tokenizer produced it.

3. Compare an answer with and without source material

  1. Choose a simple question whose answer you can check on an official website, such as a library's weekend hours.
  2. Ask the AI the question without giving it the website. Save its answer.
  3. Copy the relevant paragraph from the official website into the chat. Ask: “Using only this paragraph, answer my question.”
  4. Compare both answers with the website.

What this shows: without the source, the AI may rely on older patterns or make a plausible guess. When you provide the source, it can use that current information — but you should still check important answers.
